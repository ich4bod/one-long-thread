Choosing a picture knits all 192 stitches again.

A blank cloth, a lantern, a fern and a window use the same 192 sections and four dyes. Choosing starts a new picture; pulling still releases the very same colored strand.

Choose a color, then tap a stitch. Dyeing changes the color, not the thread.

Choose how far one tap dyes: one stitch, its connected same-color patch, its physical row, or its physical column. A patch joins only across shared edges. Undo treats the whole tap as one edit.

With One stitch chosen, drag across the editor to dye the stitches you actually touch. One drag is one Undo step. Releasing, Stop or leaving the page ends the stroke; the dyed colors stay. Keyboard taps still dye one stitch.

Undo and redo remember up to twelve dye edits. Pulling only changes how much is knitted. Knit back before undoing; choosing a picture starts a fresh dye history.

Reflect the picture across the cloth, or cycle its four dye colors. The strand still visits each physical stitch in the same order. Nothing moves while yarn is loose; Undo restores the whole picture.

Is this really knitting?

Knitted cloth is made of loops pulled through earlier loops. In weft knitting, a whole piece can come from one yarn. Unsecured loops can be pulled out from the working end: knitters call it ripping back, or frogging. Here the needle and finishing edge are left out so you can get straight to the pulling. The drawing is a loop diagram, not a simulation of yarn tension. Its 192 equal sections are counting units, not measured lengths. Our yarn is dyed section by section; this is not a lesson in changing yarn colors.
